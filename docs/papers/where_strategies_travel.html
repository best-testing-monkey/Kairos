Kairos Research Note—Market Segmentation —31 August 2026

Where Strategies Travel

Does a strategy that works on equities work on crypto? On gold? On a Hong Kong listing? Segmenting 5.6 million signals by asset class and listing venue — and finding that what makes performance portable is the forecast, not the market.

Kairos Project1d bars · 6-month window 3 asset classes · 6 listing venuescompanion to The Prediction Premium

Abstract

The companion paper treated the 127-strategy corpus as one population. It is not. Segmenting results by asset class shows large, systematic differences in level: under perfect foresight the median strategy reaches +5.56 Sharpe on FX and commodities and +2.70 on equities, but −3.35 on crypto — a spread far wider than the difference between prediction regimes.

The more useful finding concerns portability. Ranking strategies within each asset class and correlating those rankings, the no-forecast regime averages ρ = +0.28 across the three class pairs — a weak, partly-shared ordering. With a perfect forecast agreement rises to +0.74. Forecast information is a large part of what makes strategy quality a property of the strategy rather than of the market it happened to be tested on.

Listing venue matters much less than asset class. Across six exchanges, strategy rankings agree with the US ordering at ρ = +0.92 to +0.97 under oracle. A Hong Kong or German listing is, for these strategies, far more like a US listing than any equity is like a crypto pair.

One class breaks the companion paper’s pattern outright: FX and commodities are the only segment profitable without any forecast at all, at a naive median of +0.28 with 21 of 37 strategies above zero, against −0.47 on equities and −2.33 on crypto.

This edition is the first scored entirely on the unified walk-forward exit rule (companion paper, §3.4), which retires the evaluator artefact this note previously offered as a partial explanation for crypto’s deficit — the deficit survived the correction. It also comes with a gap: the base sweep is mid-re-run at 84 of 961 groups and covers only equities so far, so the middle rung of the portability ladder is missing until coverage lands.

01The question

A corpus-wide average assumes the corpus faces one market. It does not. Crypto trades continuously and reprices on sentiment; gold responds to real rates and risk appetite; an equity carries idiosyncratic earnings risk on top of its index beta; a Hong Kong listing trades in a different session, under different microstructure, than a New York one.

If strategies behave differently across those settings, then a single ranking of the corpus is misleading — it describes an average market that no one trades. The practical question is whether a strategy selected on one class can be deployed on another, or whether selection has to be redone per market.

This note segments the same three prediction regimes used in the companion paper — naive (no forecast), base (Kronos-base), oracle (perfect foresight) — by asset class and, within equities, by listing venue.

02Portability: the headline

Rank agreement answers the deployment question directly, and it is robust in a way that level comparisons are not: it does not care that crypto and equity groups contain different assets over different sessions, only whether the two markets order the strategies the same way.

Without a forecast, agreement is weak. The three class pairs average ρ = +0.28 (+0.27 equity–crypto, +0.50 equity–FX, +0.05 crypto–FX). Some ordering does survive — a strategy that is catastrophic on one class tends to be poor on another — but it is far too loose to select on, and the crypto–FX pair carries no usable signal at all. A strategy’s naive standing on equities explains under a tenth of its variance in standing on crypto.

With a perfect forecast, agreement rises sharply and consistently: oracle reaches +0.61 to +0.87 across the three pairs (mean +0.74), with the weakest pair — crypto–FX, the two thinnest classes — still more than twice its naive value. The forecast supplies a common signal that each strategy transforms in its own characteristic way, so merit becomes a property of the transformation rather than of the market. That is the finding worth carrying forward: prediction is a large part of what makes strategy selection portable.

The middle rung is missing in this edition. The base sweep is being re-run under the corrected exit rule and has reached 84 of 961 groups, all but five of them equities, so no class outside equities clears the five-group minimum and no cross-class coefficient can be computed for it. The previous edition put base at a mean of +0.71, between naive and oracle — but that figure was measured on the retired one-bar exit rule and is not comparable to the two coefficients above, which is why it is not carried forward here. The ladder has a rung missing rather than a rung guessed.

Under perfect foresight the spread is dramatic: a median of +5.56 on FX and commodities and +2.70 on equities against −3.35 on crypto. The corpus extracts far more from a perfectly forecast commodity or equity bar than from a perfectly forecast crypto bar. The mechanism is bracket geometry rather than direction: crypto’s intrabar range is wide relative to a strategy’s stop distance, so a position that is directionally right is stopped out on the way. Crypto’s mean win rate under oracle is 36.3% against equities’ 59.8% and FX/commodities’ 66.2%, all three holding a perfect next-bar forecast — direction is not what is failing.

An explanation this edition can no longer use

The previous edition attributed part of crypto’s deficit to the evaluator: oracle and base were scored one bar ahead and force-closed at that bar’s close, which penalises wide-range instruments more than a walk-forward would. That asymmetry is gone — all three regimes now walk forward to a genuine stop or target (companion paper, §3.4) and the whole corpus has been re-swept under the new rule.

Crypto’s deficit survived it. On the same ruler as everything else, crypto still sits about six Sharpe below equities under oracle, on a 36.3% win rate with perfect foresight. The wide-range explanation stands; the evaluator excuse does not. Whatever is failing on crypto is in the brackets the strategies choose, not in how those brackets were scored.

Under the base model only equities clear the five-group minimum — 80 of the 84 re-swept groups are equity — so Table 1’s base row has a single line, at +0.21. Levels are not comparable across regimes anyway, since the regimes cover different group samples. Checked properly, on the 1,597 matched (group, strategy) equity cells where all three ran, the median is +5.86 under oracle, +0.32 under base and −0.43 under naive — the same ordering the companion paper reports, on the same assets. The 37 matched crypto cells put oracle at +8.20 against base’s −0.43. The ceiling towers over the system in both.

3.1 — FX and commodities work without a forecast

The naive row carries the one result that contradicts the companion paper’s headline. That paper concludes that the corpus does not work without forecast information — the median strategy well below zero. Segmented, that conclusion holds for equities (median −0.47, 9 of 37 strategies positive) and holds harder for crypto (−2.33, 3 of 37). It fails for FX and commodities, where the naive median is +0.28 with 21 of 37 strategies positive — the only regime-and-class cell in this study that is broadly profitable with no forward information whatsoever.

The plausible reading is that these instruments are the corpus’s best fit for structural rather than directional edge. The companion paper found that the strategies surviving the naive regime were those trading bracket geometry and win-rate asymmetry rather than direction; range-bound, mean-reverting commodity and currency series are exactly where that kind of edge should persist. The same class is also the strongest under oracle at +5.56, so the structural edge and the forecast-driven one stack rather than compete.

It is by far the thinnest cell in the study — 24,892 signals from 14 groups, against 2.6 million from 928 groups for naive equities — and it got thinner in this edition, not wider. The previous version of this note reported +1.34 on 128,327 signals, drawn partly from groups outside the deduped 961-group list that a targeted backfill had added. The re-sweep covers the 961-group list only, so those extra groups are gone and both the median and the count fell with them (27 of 38 above zero, then; 21 of 37, now). The result held its sign and its uniqueness across a four-fold cut in sample; treat the magnitude as the least established number in this note.

Under oracle, every non-US venue ranks strategies almost identically to the US (ρ = +0.92 to +0.97). Whatever differences exist between trading in New York, Hong Kong, Frankfurt, Sydney, Zurich and London, they do not change which strategies work. Compare that to the +0.75 equity-versus-crypto figure and the ordering is clear: instrument type is a first-order effect; listing venue is a second-order one.

Non-US venues do show higher median Sharpe under oracle (+1.8 to +4.5 against the US +1.6), but this should be discounted heavily. Non-US signal counts are two orders of magnitude smaller (7,000–71,000 against 2.32 million), and the universe screen’s flat $50M liquidity floor is far more selective abroad than in the US — the surviving non-US names are their markets’ largest and most liquid, which is a different population, not a better market. Under naive the same venues show scattered, weak agreement (ρ from −0.05 to +0.65), reinforcing §2: strip the forecast and cross-market agreement collapses at venue level too. The base rows cover only two venues so far and are not yet worth reading.

05A defect found on the way

Eight names, one strategy

Segmenting the corpus exposed groups of strategies returning byte-identical Sharpe and signal counts. Eight names are one strategy: trend_following plus cds_spread_filter, cot_positioning_filter, dark_pool_filter, fractal_dimension, gaussian_process, insider_cluster and onchain_flow_filter.

Each of the seven filters wraps TrendFollowingStrategy() and gates on a context key the pipeline never supplies — cds_spread_change, dark_pool_sentiment, cot_net_position and so on. Each read defaults to 0.0, every gate condition is therefore false, and each filter degrades to an unmodified pass-through that differs from trend_following only in the name stamped on the signal. This is confirmed in code, not inferred from the numbers.

The consequence is that corpus-wide statistics count one behaviour eight times. Since that behaviour is unprofitable in every regime and sits near the middle of the oracle distribution, removing the seven duplicates moves the medians — substantially, in oracle’s case.

A wider audit is warranted. Several further near-duplicate pairs coincide on most but not all groups (expected_value/vol_target_sizer on 445 groups, range_trading/rqa_determinism on 302, dynamic_bracket/inverse_variance on 262, amount_flow/predicted_vwap on 70). Those are not exact aliases, but they are close enough to warrant checking whether they are meaningfully distinct strategies.

06Limitations

  1. Levels are not comparable across regimes. The three sweeps cover different groups per class, so §3’s within-regime class comparisons are valid while any cross-regime reading of the same table is not. Where a cross-regime claim was needed, it was computed on matched cells and is labelled as such.
  2. The base regime is mid-re-sweep and covers one class. 84 of 961 groups have been re-run under the current exit rule, 80 of them equity, so base has no cross-class rank coefficient (§2), no divergence table (§3.2), and only two listing venues (§4). Everything this note says about the base model is provisional and rests on equities. Oracle and naive are complete at 961 of 961 groups each and are not affected.
  3. Superseded: the exit-rule asymmetry is gone. Earlier editions carried the companion paper’s caveat that oracle and base were scored one bar ahead then force-closed while naive walked forward multi-bar, and noted that this bore directly on §3’s crypto explanation — a single-bar forced close penalises wide-range instruments, so part of crypto’s oracle deficit might have been evaluator artefact. All three regimes now share one walk-forward rule and the corpus has been re-swept under it. Crypto’s deficit persisted, so that particular doubt is resolved rather than outstanding (§3).
  4. Class samples are very unequal. Under oracle, equities contribute 748 groups per strategy against 25 for crypto and 12 for FX/commodities. The crypto and FX medians rest on far less data, and no confidence intervals are computed anywhere in this note. FX/commodity coverage is smaller than the previous edition’s because the re-sweep covers the deduped 961-group list only (§7.1).
  5. Superseded: the FX/commodity sample is no longer commodity-only. An earlier version of this note warned that seventeen genuine FX-pair groups (EURUSD=X, CADJPY=X, …) classified as unknown and were dropped, because class was derived from the universe screen’s membership list and those symbols were never screened. Class now comes from per-signal attribution recorded at sweep time, which classifies by ticker suffix and so places them correctly — recovering 222,342 previously-discarded oracle signals and roughly doubling this class’s coverage. §3.1 now reflects both currencies and commodities. It remains the thinnest class in the study.
  6. Venue is a listing suffix, not an economy. Groups are classified by yfinance symbol suffix, which identifies where a security is listed — not where the company earns, nor its sector. No sector or country-of-revenue data exists in the pipeline, so “the market the company is in” is only approximated, and multinationals are misattributed by construction.
  7. Mixed-class and mixed-venue groups are excluded rather than modelled, along with the small number of groups containing symbols absent from the universe screen. This is a clean-sample choice, not a claim that mixed baskets behave like either constituent.
  8. No costs, shadow accounting, no significance testing — all as described in the companion paper, and all still true here.

07Data provenance

Same database and tables as the companion paper. The only addition is the symbol→class join, which comes from the universe screen rather than from the results tables.

-- asset class per symbol (1d universe run 759: 1,779 equity, 52 crypto, 20 fx_commodity)
SELECT symbol, asset_class FROM universe_screen WHERE passed=1 AND run_id=759;

-- a group's class is the class of its members; mixed and unknown groups are dropped
-- a group's venue is the yfinance suffix (no suffix => US); mixed-venue groups dropped

-- per-signal class attribution, recorded at sweep time
SELECT s.strategy_name, s.sharpe, s.signal_count, s.win_rate, s.avg_pnl_per_trade,
       s.assets, s.asset_class
FROM   strategy_class_stats s JOIN runs r ON r.run_id = s.run_id
WHERE  s.stage = ?  AND s.signal_count >= 3  AND s.asset_class != 'mixed'
  AND  r.timestamp >= '2026-08-30';   -- the one-exit-rule re-sweep; earlier rows
                                      -- use the retired one-bar rule and are excluded

Per-strategy figures are signal-count-weighted within each class, requiring at least 5 groups per strategy per class. Rank correlations use one common strategy set per regime, so the coefficients within a regime are comparable to each other. Scripts: analyze_by_market3.py (analysis), build_market_report.py (this page). 7.1 — Coverage, and what the re-sweep changed

Every figure in this edition comes from the re-sweep that followed the exit-rule unification of 29 August 2026. Rows written before it were scored one bar ahead and force-closed, so they are excluded rather than blended in — which also means this note’s footprint is now exactly the deduped 961-group list, for oracle and naive alike, with no groups from outside it. Contributing groups per class: 928 equity, 31 crypto, 14 FX/commodity (a group can contribute signals to more than one class). Base stands at 84 groups: 80 equity, 4 crypto, 1 FX/commodity.

Superseded: the naive backfill. Earlier editions of this note described a targeted backfill that ran naive over 186 groups from outside the deduped list, taking it from 959 to 1,144 groups and lifting crypto from 29 to 99 groups and FX/commodities from 4 to 33. Those rows are pre-fix and no longer used. The class coverage above is therefore narrower than the previous edition’s, and §3.1’s FX/commodity result is measured on roughly a quarter of the signals it previously rested on. It survived the cut; it is the thinnest claim here. Extending the sweeps beyond the deduped list on the current exit rule would restore the width.

The list itself is 95% equity by construction — it is a greedy set cover over universe survivors, and those are 1,779 equity against 52 crypto and 20 FX/commodity — so no amount of re-sweeping the list alone will balance the classes. Sweep command:

uv run scripts/run_oracle_dedup.py --stage oracle|naive --workers 8
uv run scripts/run_base_priority.py                            (base)

08Conclusion

Strategies do not perform the same across markets, but the differences are not where intuition puts them. Asset class produces large, systematic gaps in achievable performance — the FX-to-crypto spread under perfect foresight is nearly nine Sharpe, wider than the gap between having a forecast and not having one. Listing venue produces almost none: a London or Hong Kong equity ranks strategies essentially as a US equity does.

The finding with the most practical weight is that portability is substantially improved by prediction. With no forecast, per-class rankings agree only weakly (mean ρ = +0.28) and a strategy selected on one market would largely have to be re-selected on the next. With a perfect forecast agreement rises to +0.74 and selection made on one class carries much better to another. That extends the companion paper’s conclusion: the model does not only lift performance, it makes performance mean more nearly the same thing in different markets. Whether the real model does as much of that as the perfect one is the measurement this edition is missing, and it returns with base coverage.

One class dissents. FX and commodities are profitable with no forecast at all, the only such cell here, which suggests the corpus holds a structural edge there that is independent of prediction quality — worth isolating, and worth testing on a wider currency sample than the fourteen groups available here.

The practical recommendations are to treat asset class as a first-class dimension in strategy selection while treating venue as second-order, to investigate the strategies that reverse sign between classes before deploying any of them, and to fix the aliasing in §5 before the next corpus-wide sweep so that eight names stop voting eight times.
